A baseline, not a leaderboard

20 August 2026 · 5 min read

An RL agent is almost always compared to something. Usually to another agent on a leaderboard: PPO against SAC, +12% on Atari. For research that is fine. For an operational problem — pricing, a warehouse, a queue — it is almost useless. The question is not which agent is newer. The question is whether a neural network beats the method the field has used for fifty years.

This is the whole idea behind decisionrl: not a bigger algorithm zoo, but a way to check whether an algorithm was worth reaching for at all.

The baseline is the method that already works

An inventory problem has a base solution: the base-stock policy. It is decades old, it fits in two lines, and on stationary demand it is provably optimal. If your RL loses to base-stock, you have built a complicated way to do worse than a calculator. If it wins by one percent, the gap drowns in a single week of demand noise.

So every applied environment in the library ships with its classical operations-research baseline: base-stock for inventory, a value threshold for the queue, a greedy policy for the battery, a per-echelon base-stock for the supply chain. A policy is measured against it, not asserted to be good.

Two honest outcomes, kept apart

Where a closed-form optimum exists, RL should recover it, not beat it. On stationary inventory the learned policy reaches 194.5 against the exact dynamic-programming optimum of 196.1 — indistinguishable, which is the correct result. Pricing lands at 25.3 against the best fixed rule's 25.5. Matching a known optimum is a pass: the agent found the answer on its own, and there is nothing to pay a network for.

Where the classical method breaks, RL earns its place. Make demand nonstationary — two regimes, a 5% chance of switching, holding and stockout costs that punish a wrong fixed level — and a single base-stock is no longer optimal. Over four seeds DQN holds 319–329 and beats the best fixed base-stock at roughly 322 versus 257: about +25%.

PPO is unstable on this task — one seed collapses to 130, another holds 308 — so the honest claim uses DQN, the algorithm that is stable across seeds, not the average of a lucky run.

Measured against real software, not a quote

The comparison is against a real Stable-Baselines3, run on the same tasks, not a number lifted from a paper. PPO reaches parity on CartPole, 500/500. On the drifting-inventory task the library's DQN scores 327 ± 122 against SB3's 96 ± 57.

Under the hood: 31 algorithms, 22 environments — nine of them operational — 400 tests, 86% coverage, typed and checked with mypy, CI on Python 3.9 through 3.12, published on PyPI as decisionrl. The numbers in the README are pinned by tests, because prose rots quietly and a failing test does not.

When not to use RL

If a closed form exists and demand is stationary, use it: the solver is faster, exact, and needs no training. RL is for the cases the solver cannot state — coupled decisions, nonstationarity, costs without a clean formula.

The rule is short: compute the baseline first, then train the agent. With nothing to compare against, you do not know whether it works.
